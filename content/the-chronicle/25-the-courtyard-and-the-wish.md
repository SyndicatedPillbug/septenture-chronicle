# 25 — The Courtyard and the Wish

The courtyard outside the Whites did not end the way anyone expected, and it did not end because of the militia.

It opened the way the last hour had left it: fog in the gutters, star-brick underfoot, a line of black-coated militia pressed into the alley mouth and three war-hounds coming off the leash. Sergeant Rell Hask came with them, and for a moment the black-line had the numbers and the nerve to use them. Ceridwen was on her feet again — Coy had seen to that at the very end of the last session — and she stood up out of the dirt, marked the sergeant, and let a dolorous bell toll somewhere behind his eyes. Then she laid a hex on his strength and smiled the way she does when she is frightened and does not intend to say so.

But the hounds were the real problem. One took Page by the leg and dropped him flat, and the gale he had been holding down the alley — the wind that had pinned the whole patrol — tore loose and died. The courtyard went suddenly, terribly quiet.

After that it was a knife-fight in the fog.

Selim carved into the sergeant and the hounds with a soldier's economy, then surged and did it all again when the moment asked for more. Legion bloomed a grey mist across the alley mouth and stood in the edge of it, where nothing could see them but them. Coy wore the bear and called down a spectral pack of his own, and the pack savaged the swordsman and the halberdier both. The militia broke under it. The halberdier fled bleeding down the alley after Ceridwen whispered a discordant little nothing into his ear. The Blade and the sergeant watched their line come apart and did the professional thing: they dropped their swords and opened their hands.

That was when Brendon's stranger stepped out of the fog and refused the surrender.

She put a round of fire into the Blade, and then a second into Hask, and did not stop because they had already stopped fighting. Two prisoners with their hands open, and she shot them anyway. Nobody in the courtyard missed what it meant. Nobody said anything about it either, because there are things you save for later, and the later is always longer than you think.

Then Ceridwen did something nobody saw coming.

She reached for the power she had spent months being afraid of — the cold at her wrist, the whisper that never quite stops — and this time she did not flinch. She took Morgath's hand. She spoke a wish, in a voice that was half hers and half something much older, and the courtyard simply stopped: every soldier, every hound, every raised blade and ragged breath, dropped where it stood. The fog hung motionless in the air as though the world had forgotten how to move.

The fight was over.

Only Coy was awake to see what it cost. He felt the wish coming and fought it — bear-muscle and rage — and it took him anyway; but in the half-second before the grey closed over him, he saw. A low, patient shape at Ceridwen's shoulder, an unlit weight with an appetite behind it: Morgath, wearing a shape of his own at last. And across the courtyard, where Page lay in the fog, a light — gold and slow, welling up from under his skin like something underneath finally waking.

Then the dark took him too.

---

They woke on a cliff.

A ledge of wet rock high above the sea, with the night wind coming off the water and the whole city of Latesa glowing below them — the Basin's lamps, the Rungs, the long grey seam of the cliff face going down and down into its own light. Somewhere out past the breakwater the ocean breathed and did not care.

Coy was human again. Selim was not there. The gunslinger was, standing a little apart with the sea in her face and her hands loose at her sides, like a woman who has decided to be patient about something and is not yet sure what.

Nobody had a good explanation. The wish had taken the field, and then it had taken *them*, and it had set them down here on the cold side of the cliff with the city they had spent the last month bleeding in spread out beneath them like a map of somebody else's trouble. The militia were still in the courtyard, asleep or dead or waking into either. The dispatch case was still unopened. The halberdier was still loose in the fog.

And the two people they had promised to protect were still behind a dry fountain in the middle of all of it.

Page sat down on the ledge because his leg would not hold him, and because the wind was cold and honest and he wanted, for a moment, to feel something that was only weather. The city glowed. The sea went on. Somewhere below, a lamp was burning in a cliffside window, and a curtain moved, and someone put a pot on to scrape.

That was the light they followed: one lamp, one curtain, one pot put on to scrape.
