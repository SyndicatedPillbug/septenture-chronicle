# 2.2 — The Salt Mare's Fortune

<!-- source: content/the-chronicle/c2-02-the-salt-mares-fortune.md | dateline: the southern sea, 1396cr | description: A dead ship with a living crew, an invasion rumor, and a port that will not let them in. -->

*the southern sea, 1396cr*



The voyage ran between nineteen and thirty days, depending on the weather, which gave the party time to learn the crew's names, the cook's limitations, and the way sailors talk on the night watch.

Ceridwen made a friend of the sailor Treza Jambad, who had stories and a willingness to spend them. The best of them was about the Gilded Hind, found adrift off the Norforth Coast three summers back. She looked perfect from the rail. Sails full, lanterns lit in broad daylight, not a scratch on her. Nobody on the ship had died of plague or pirates; the whole crew stood on the deck where they had been standing when it happened, frozen and bloodless with their mouths open and one arm raised, every one of them pointing at the empty crow's nest. The wizard who tried to read the death rolled his eyes back and wept black tar. They burned the Hind where she drifted. Treza said he still checked his own crow's nest on quiet watches, out of respect.

The crew argued about Danruian. Some had heard the islands were massing an invasion fleet; others had heard the same story every year for a decade. Simon gave out presents he had been saving for the right audience, which bought him goodwill in every mess table on the ship. Page drew up a list of ingredients for Coy and Legion to hunt down once they were on solid ground, and put feathers, gum arabic, and a spider on it, which says most of what needs saying about the man.

On the day the coast came back, the wind did not so much fail as decide against them. The city rose out of the haze in layers, a cliff covered in buildings, and the smoke of the Shave sitting on top of it like a hand. The docks were closed. A blockade of writs and soldiers turned the ship away from the harbor and sent it along to a fishing beach to the east, where the passengers could walk.

So they walked. The cliff trail ran above the water for the last miles, past the Great Ox-Elevators descending into the rock with their cargo, and down to the inland gate. The gate was still taking travelers, and the queue moved at the speed of paperwork.

The talk in the queue carried the city's news past them while they waited. Murders in the Shave. People gone missing out of the cliffside warrens, year after year, with nobody counting them. Nobody said either thing too loudly, and nobody had to.

When their turn came, the guards looked at five tired travelers with a mule and found nothing worth writing down. Above the wall, the city climbed the cliff in steps, with a brown smear of refinery smog over its far end.
